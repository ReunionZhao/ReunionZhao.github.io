Introduction

Experiences, Information and Attitudes toward Refugees

with Pedro Bordalo and Sandra Sequeira

Updated: October 2026

Paper Slides

We study whether information can shift attitudes towards refugees, how long such shifts last, and what cognitive processes drive them. In a two-wave online experiment with approximately 1,200 UK respondents, we randomly expose participants to a documentary film, an academic paper, or both, each presenting evidence on the challenges and opportunities of refugee integration in Sub-Saharan Africa. All treatments generated more favorable attitudes toward refugees in the UK (including attitudes about local integration, which are typically more resistant to change) with effects persisting up to 8 months after the intervention. The documentary generates significantly more durable attitude change than the paper, particularly in the longer run. Using free-recall text analyzed through LLM-based classifications, we show that the documentary’s lasting advantage operates through recategorization: documentary viewers encode more, retain more, and shift from a “conflict” to an “empathy” mental model of refugees, and this retained knowledge predicts their attitudes months later. Exposure also reduces support for immigrants more broadly — a cross-domain spillover of comparable magnitude to the pro-refugee effect. This spillover is driven almost entirely by immigrant respondents, who process the same content through a “competition” mental model, and update negatively on their own group as being less deserving, rather than positively on refugees. Together, our findings establish three results: attitudes on salient political issues are malleable when information reaches people through the right medium; medium matters independently of content, because it shapes how deeply information is encoded and retained; and prior lived experience determines not just what people learn but the mental model through which they interpret new information and which attitudinal domain they update.

Research Miscellanea

Beyond my “official” agenda, I am also curious about the “science of science”: how knowledge is produced and spread. I keep returning to this meta view. Why do some research questions attract people for a lifetime and gather whole communities around them, while others are quickly solved or forgotten? I am also interested in why the research frontier inherently depends on tacit knowledge and taste. These are hard to write down and travel only through close interaction. I wonder how such “tacit assets” determine who can produce and recognize new knowledge, and where it ends up concentrated. Maybe one day it will become a paper!
